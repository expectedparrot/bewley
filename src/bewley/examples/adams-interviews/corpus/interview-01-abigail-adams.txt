Status: FICTIONAL — synthetic demonstration data written in 2026 for the

INTERVIEWER: Mrs. Adams, thank you for sitting with me. I'd like to start
with the autumn of 1775. Your husband was in Philadelphia at the Congress.
What did an ordinary day in Braintree look like for you?

ABIGAIL ADAMS: There was very little that was ordinary about it. I had the
children, the farm, the accounts, and the schooling to manage, and no
certainty from one week to the next what the army at Boston would do. The
merchant complains of the farmer and the farmer of the merchant, and I
stood between them trying to keep the household fed. I did not choose
those duties; they arrived when John departed, and they did not ask my
leave.

INTERVIEWER: You wrote to him constantly through that period. What did the
letters carry that the newspapers could not?

ABIGAIL ADAMS: Everything that mattered. The papers carried proclamations;
my letters carried the price of pins and the health of his children. And
his to me carried too little, if I am honest — I told him more than once
that a letter half as long as I wished was still a feast after three weeks
of silence. When the post failed, my heart was as heavy as lead. You
cannot understand the war between us and Britain without understanding
the smaller war against distance itself.

INTERVIEWER: In March of 1776 you asked him to "remember the ladies" in
the new code of laws. Did you expect the request to be taken seriously?

ABIGAIL ADAMS: I expected exactly what I received, which was laughter
dressed as affection. But I was in earnest. All men would be tyrants if
they could — I wrote that plainly. If particular care is not paid to the
ladies, we are determined to foment a rebellion of our own, and we will
not hold ourselves bound by laws in which we have no voice. That I said
it with some wit does not mean I did not mean it.

INTERVIEWER: Were you afraid, those first summers of the war?

ABIGAIL ADAMS: We lived in continual expectation of alarms. I heard the
cannon at Boston from my own hill. Fear was a boarder in every house on
that coast; the question was only whether you let it govern the
household. I did not.
