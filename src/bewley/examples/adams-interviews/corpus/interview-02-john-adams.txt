INTERVIEWER: Mr. Adams, you spent most of 1775 and 1776 away from your
family, at the Congress in Philadelphia. How did you justify that absence
to yourself?

JOHN ADAMS: Justify is the right word, because it required justification
daily. I believed — I still believe — that a man who is offered a part in
the founding of a government has no honest way to refuse it. But I will
not pretend the ledger balanced. My wife governed a farm through a war
while I governed nothing but committees. When Henry was made a General, I
remember thinking that every man in America seemed to have a commission
except the ones sitting in that room, and that ours was the only work
nobody would ever see.

INTERVIEWER: The second of July, 1776 — you wrote that it would be
celebrated as the most memorable epoch in the history of America. Did it
feel that large in the moment?

JOHN ADAMS: Larger. I wrote to Abigail that it ought to be commemorated
with pomp and parade, with shows, games, sports, guns, bells, bonfires,
and illuminations, from one end of this continent to the other. People
now smile that I fixed on the wrong date. Let them smile — the point was
never the date. The point was that thirteen quarrelsome colonies had done
an irreversible thing, and I wanted my wife to know it from my hand
before she knew it from a crier.

INTERVIEWER: Your letters home are affectionate but often short. Your
wife noticed.

JOHN ADAMS: She did, and she was right to. I answered her in the
tenderest language I possessed, but I answered late and I answered
briefly, and a committee has no tenderness to spare. The truth is that
her letters were the better history. Mine reported votes; hers reported
a country. If posterity reads only one side of that correspondence, I
know which side I would advise.

INTERVIEWER: Did the distance change the marriage?

JOHN ADAMS: It taught us to conduct a marriage by post, which is a thing
I recommend to no one. Every argument arrived three weeks stale and
every comfort likewise. And yet I do not know two people who wrote
themselves closer together. The separation was the price of the duty;
the letters were how we refused to pay it entirely.
